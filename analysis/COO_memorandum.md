You asked us how an open waiting area can provide relief while controlling energy use and cost. Our analysis of Boston?s Grove Hall bus shelter points to a practical recommendation: operate cooling according to passenger conditions. Use the fan when it provides enough relief, consider the larger evaporative cooler when stronger cooling is needed, and switch equipment off when conditions already meet the comfort target.

We compared three cooling systems and no device under peak-temperature, hot-humid, and hot-dry scenarios. We measured comfort using Standard Effective Temperature (SET), which accounts for temperature, humidity, air movement, and other influences. Our chosen comparison range is 23?26?C SET.

The hot-humid scenario shows where the fan offers the best value. It lowers predicted SET from 27.66?C to 25.09?C, reaching our target while using 0.25 kWh per hour and no water. Both evaporative coolers also reach the target, but require more resources. We recommend piloting the fan for these conditions: compared with the larger cooler, it uses 90% less electricity and avoids approximately 44 kg of evaporated water per hour. Its reference purchase price is $1,499, versus $4,939 for the larger cooler, before installation, tax, and maintenance.

During peak heat, the larger cooler provides the greatest relief, reducing SET from 30.77?C to 28.57?C. However, this is only 0.49?C below the fan?s result and requires ten times the electricity plus approximately 81 kg of evaporated water per hour. Before purchasing it, establish electricity, water, and spending limits and rerun the model. Our current decision rule selects the larger cooler to minimize worst-case hot-discomfort regret, with those resource limits disabled.

Avoid unnecessary operation. In the hot-dry scenario, no device already achieves 24.44?C SET, while all active options fall below our comparison range. Monitor conditions where passengers wait, reduce cooling when it is unnecessary, and maintain evaporative reservoirs and cooling media regularly.

Finally, none of the systems reaches our target during peak heat. Maintain an extreme-heat response plan with access to an air-conditioned refuge and clear passenger information. Our comfort target is not a safety threshold, and predictions rely on estimated airflow and a reference passenger. Verify performance through a site trial before wider deployment. This approach gives operators a clear starting point: provide the relief passengers need, check that it works, and spend additional resources where they deliver worthwhile benefits.
